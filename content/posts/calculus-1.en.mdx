Training a machine learning model is ultimately about **minimizing a loss function**. The mathematical tool that makes this minimization possible is the **derivative**. In this post, we'll build intuition for what a derivative means, then work through the differentiation rules for the most important functions.

## Why Do We Need Derivatives in Machine Learning?

Training a machine learning model is an **optimization** problem.

- **Regression**: find the line that best explains the data points
- **Classification**: find the decision boundary that best separates the data

In both cases, we start from an arbitrary line and gradually **tweak** it toward the best fit. This process is called **optimization**, and derivatives tell us which direction to move and by how much.

---

## What Is a Derivative? — Instantaneous Rate of Change

Think of a car's speedometer. You can easily calculate the **average speed** over a full trip.
<br />
$$\text{Average speed} = \frac{\Delta \text{distance}}{\Delta \text{time}} = \frac{y(t_2) - y(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$$
<br />
But how do you find the **speed at a specific instant**? You shrink the time interval down to zero.
<br />
$$\text{Instantaneous speed} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}$$
<br />
That is exactly the **derivative** — the instantaneous rate of change of a function.

**Example:** Average speed between $t = 10$ and $t = 15$ seconds
<br />
$$v = \frac{y(15) - y(10)}{15 - 10} = \frac{202 - 122}{5} = 16 \text{ m/s}$$
<br />
Narrowing to $t = 12$ to $t = 13$ seconds gives an approximation of the instantaneous speed at $t = 12.5$ seconds.

> **Key analogy**: distance = function $f(x)$, speed = derivative $f'(x)$

---

## Tangent Lines and Derivatives

The **instantaneous rate of change** is equal to the **slope of the tangent line** at a point on the curve.

A tangent line touches the curve at exactly one point without crossing through it.
<br />
$$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$$
<br />
$$\boxed{\text{Derivative} = \text{Slope of the tangent line}}$$

---

## Slope, Maxima, and Minima

Where do a function's maximum and minimum values occur?

**Core principle:** A function's maximum or minimum occurs at a point where the derivative equals zero (slope = 0).
<br />
$$f'(x) = 0 \Rightarrow \text{candidate for maximum or minimum}$$

| Derivative sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $f'(x) > 0$ | Function is **increasing** |
| $f'(x) = 0$ | Slope is zero — candidate for extremum (max/min) |
| $f'(x) < 0$ | Function is **decreasing** |

> **In practice:** The point where the loss function's derivative equals zero corresponds to the model's optimal parameters. Gradient descent moves toward that point step by step.

## Derivative Notation

There are several common ways to write a derivative.

| Notation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $f'(x)$ | Lagrange notation |
| $\dfrac{df}{dx}$ | Leibniz notation |
| $\dot{f}$ | Newton notation (typically used for time derivatives) |

## Derivatives of Common Functions

### Constant Function

$$f(x) = c \Rightarrow f'(x) = 0$$
<br />
A constant never changes, so its rate of change is zero.

### Linear Function

$$f(x) = ax + b \Rightarrow f'(x) = a$$
<br />
The slope $a$ is constant everywhere, so the derivative equals $a$ at every point. The constant term $b$ has no effect on the rate of change.

### Quadratic Function

$$f(x) = x^2 \Rightarrow f'(x) = 2x$$
<br />
**Intuitive derivation:**
<br />
$$\frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2}{\Delta x} = \frac{2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2}{\Delta x} = 2x + \Delta x \xrightarrow{\Delta x \to 0} 2x$$

### Higher-Degree Polynomials (Power Rule)

$$f(x) = x^n \Rightarrow f'(x) = nx^{n-1}$$
<br />
**Rule:** Bring the exponent down as a coefficient, then reduce the exponent by one.

| Function | Derivative |
|---|---|
| $x^3$ | $3x^2$ |
| $x^4$ | $4x^3$ |
| $x^{-1}$ | $-x^{-2}$ |
| $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$ |

---

## Derivatives of Inverse Functions

An inverse function **undoes** what the original function did.
<br />
$$g = f^{-1} \Rightarrow g'(x) = \frac{1}{f'(g(x))}$$
<br />
**The derivative of an inverse function is the reciprocal of the original function's derivative.**

---

## Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) = \cos(x)$$
<br />
$$\frac{d}{dx}\cos(x) = -\sin(x)$$
<br />
$$\frac{d}{dx}\tan(x) = \sec^2(x)$$
<br />
**Intuition:** As $\Delta x$ approaches zero, the hypotenuse of the triangle converges to $\Delta x$, and working through the algebra yields the trig differentiation formulas.

---

## Euler's Number $e$ and the Derivative of Exponential Functions

### What Is $e$?

Using compound interest as an analogy:
<br />
$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828\ldots$$
<br />
As $n$ grows larger, the expression converges to $e$.

### Derivative of $e^x$

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$$
<br />
$e^x$ is the only function that is **its own derivative** — differentiation leaves it unchanged.

> **In practice:** The derivative of the sigmoid function $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ is central to backpropagation in deep learning.

---

## Derivative of the Logarithmic Function

$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln(x) = \frac{1}{x}$$
<br />
**Why:** $\ln(x)$ is the inverse of $e^x$, so by the inverse function rule, its derivative is $\frac{1}{x}$.

---

## When a Derivative Does Not Exist

Not every function is differentiable at every point.

| Case | Example | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Cusp** | $\|x\|$ at $x=0$ | Left derivative ≠ right derivative |
| **Jump discontinuity** | Step function | Function is discontinuous at that point |
| **Vertical tangent** | $x^{1/3}$ at $x=0$ | Slope = $\infty$ (division by zero) |

Functions like these are called **non-differentiable functions**.

> **In practice:** The ReLU activation $\max(0, x)$ is non-differentiable at $x=0$, but in practice the network uses a **subgradient** approximation — treating the derivative as either 0 or 1 — to keep training working.

## The Four Core Differentiation Properties

### 1. Scalar Multiplication Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}[c \cdot f(x)] = c \cdot f'(x)$$
<br />
Multiplying a function by a constant multiplies its derivative by the same constant.
<br />
$$\frac{d}{dx}[5x^3] = 5 \cdot 3x^2 = 15x^2$$

### 2. Sum Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) + g(x)] = f'(x) + g'(x)$$
<br />
The derivative of a sum equals the sum of the derivatives.
<br />
$$f(x) = 2x,\ g(x) = x^2 \Rightarrow (f+g)'(x) = 2 + 2x$$
<br />
**Intuition:** A child running on a moving train — their speed relative to the ground equals the train's speed plus the child's speed.

### 3. Product Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x)$$
<br />
**Example:** $f(x) = xe^x$
<br />
$$f'(x) = (x)' \cdot e^x + x \cdot (e^x)' = e^x + xe^x = e^x(1+x)$$

### 4. Chain Rule

The differentiation rule for composite functions.
<br />
$$\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$
<br />
**Intuition:** If you know how altitude affects temperature and how altitude changes over time:
<br />
$$\frac{d\text{temperature}}{d\text{time}} = \frac{d\text{temperature}}{d\text{altitude}} \times \frac{d\text{altitude}}{d\text{time}}$$
<br />
**Example:** $f(x) = e^{2x}$
<br />
$$g(x) = 2x,\quad f(g) = e^g$$
<br />
$$f'(x) = e^{2x} \cdot (2x)' = 2e^{2x}$$

> **In practice:** Deep learning's **backpropagation** algorithm is essentially the repeated application of the chain rule to compute the gradient at each layer.

---

## Summary of Differentiation Formulas

| Function | Derivative |
|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ |
| $x^n$ | $nx^{n-1}$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $\ln(x)$ | $\dfrac{1}{x}$ |
| $\sin(x)$ | $\cos(x)$ |
| $\cos(x)$ | $-\sin(x)$ |
| $\tan(x)$ | $\sec^2(x)$ |

| Rule | Formula |
|---|---|
| Scalar multiplication | $(cf)' = cf'$ |
| Sum rule | $(f+g)' = f' + g'$ |
| Product rule | $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$ |
| Chain rule | $(f \circ g)' = f'(g) \cdot g'$ |

**Q1. Differentiate the following function.**
<br />
$$f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x^2 + 5x - 7$$
<br />
<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Apply the sum rule, scalar multiplication rule, and power rule:
<br />
$$f'(x) = 12x^3 - 4x + 5$$

---

**Q2. Differentiate $f(x) = x^2 e^x$.**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Product rule: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$
<br />
$$f'(x) = 2x \cdot e^x + x^2 \cdot e^x = e^x(2x + x^2) = xe^x(x+2)$$
<br />
</details>

---

**Q3. Differentiate $f(x) = \ln(x^2 + 1)$.**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Chain rule: $g(x) = x^2 + 1$, $f(g) = \ln(g)$
<br />
$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2+1} \cdot 2x = \frac{2x}{x^2+1}$$
<br />
</details>

---

**Q4. Which of the following functions is NOT differentiable at $x = 0$?**

1. $f(x) = x^2$
2. $f(x) = |x|$
3. $f(x) = e^x$
4. $f(x) = \sin(x)$

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Option 2: $f(x) = |x|$**

At $x = 0$, the left-hand derivative is $-1$ and the right-hand derivative is $+1$ — they differ, so the derivative does not exist. (This is a cusp.)

</details>

---

**Q5. Differentiate $f(x) = \sin(3x^2)$.**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Apply the chain rule twice: $h(x) = 3x^2$, $g(h) = \sin(h)$
<br />
$$f'(x) = \cos(3x^2) \cdot 6x = 6x\cos(3x^2)$$
<br />
</details>
